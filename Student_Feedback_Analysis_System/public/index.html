THE CHALLENGE

Feedback is valuable.
Finding the signal is hard.

At the end of a term, a college can collect hundreds of thoughtful comments. Reading every one takes time, and patterns across teaching, course content, facilities, and assessment can be easy to miss.

Sentiment analysis helps organize that feedback. It does not replace a teacher’s judgment—it gives them a useful first overview of what students are saying.

Read between the words.

The base paper, by Abdi et al. (2023), combines neural networks with linguistic knowledge. Each part helps the model understand a different piece of a sentence.

A KNOWN CHALLENGE

Word2vec has limits

“Like” and “dislike” can appear close in vector space, and one word can have several meanings. Sentiment lexicons and context help address this.

GOOD QUESTIONS

Curious? Start here.

A plain-language guide to the project, the research, and what comes next.

What is this project?

A system that aims to classify student feedback as positive or negative and show teachers a useful summary. It follows a 2023 research paper and explores additional features.

Why analyse student feedback?

Reading hundreds of comments manually is slow, so some feedback may be missed. Summaries can help teachers spot recurring strengths and concerns.

How is deep learning different from traditional machine learning?

In traditional machine learning, a person often chooses the features. Deep-learning networks learn useful patterns from examples.

Why combine CNN, BiLSTM, and attention?

A CNN finds nearby phrase patterns, a BiLSTM reads context in both directions, and attention emphasizes the words that matter. The paper reports better results after adding attention.

How does it handle “not good” or “good, but…”?

Negation words can reverse a sentiment clue, while contrast words such as “but” divide the sentence into parts and give the final clause more weight. The current demo uses simple rules for these patterns.

What data will be used?

The plan is to use the public student-feedback dataset on Kaggle that the paper used, plus a small college survey collected through Google Forms with appropriate consent and data handling.

What is new compared with the paper?

We plan to compare contextual DistilBERT embeddings, show results by course aspect, and provide a web dashboard for analysis.

Why DistilBERT instead of BERT?

DistilBERT is smaller and faster, making it more practical to experiment with on a regular laptop or a free Google Colab runtime while retaining much of BERT’s capability.

What accuracy will the project achieve?

We do not know yet. The paper reports 88.78%; our own results will be measured and reported after training and evaluation.

Is the deep-learning model implemented already?

Not yet. The project is currently at the planning and prototype stage. The website’s analysis is a rule-based demonstration, not a trained neural model.

What are the current research limitations?

The paper’s data comes mainly from one source, supports English, and focuses on two sentiment classes. Neutral feedback and regional languages are possible future work.

What are accuracy, precision, recall, and F1?

Accuracy is the share of all predictions that are correct. Precision measures how many predicted positives are truly positive; recall measures how many real positives were found. F1 combines precision and recall.

What tools and hardware are planned?

Python with TensorFlow/Keras for model experiments, Flask for a dashboard service, and a laptop with an i5 processor and 8 GB of RAM; a free Colab GPU can help with training.

What happens next?

Collect and clean data, build a baseline, train the proposed model, add the extensions, build out the dashboard, and compare the results.

Where could this be useful?

Colleges could review feedback at the end of a term, and online courses could use summaries to find topics students struggle with.
